What is a sound bath? It's a session where you lie down, close your eyes and let the sound of singing bowls, gongs and other instruments wash over you, usually for about an hour. There's no water involved: you bathe in sound.

Does it actually help you relax? Many people say yes, and a handful of small studies found lower tension and anxiety after a session. The evidence is still early, so here is what we know and what we don't.

## What is a sound bath, exactly

A sound bath is a meditation led by instruments rather than words. One person plays while the group listens, lying on mats with blankets and pillows. Your only job is to listen.

Anthropologist Elisa J. Sobo of San Diego State University describes them in [a beginner's guide for The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/a-beginners-guide-to-sound-baths-what-they-are-how-to-choose-a-good-one-and-what-the-research-shows-220024) as intimate, hour-long, small-group events. In her view, the sound bath grew out of modern yoga: think of it as a stretched-out *savasana*, the final rest of a class, with sound added.

That context is useful. A sound bath isn't an ancient ceremony and it isn't a treatment. It's a recent relaxation practice that a lot of people enjoy.

## Bowls, gongs and drums: what you'll hear

Every facilitator builds a session differently. According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/sound-bath), the usual instruments are:

| Instrument | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| Tibetan (metal) singing bowls | A humming tone that fades slowly |
| Crystal singing bowls | A cleaner, longer tone |
| Gong | Long waves that swell and recede |
| Tuning forks and chimes | Short, bright notes |
| Drums | A low rhythm you feel in your body |

A "singing bowl meditation" is usually this same thing. A gong bath is the same idea with the gong in the lead.

Why can sound move us so much? Northwestern University neuroscientist Nina Kraus argues in her book [*Of Sound Mind* (MIT Press, 2021)](https://brainvolts.northwestern.edu/of-sound-mind/) that hearing is tied to how we think, feel and move. In her words: "The sounds of our lives shape our brains, for better and for worse."

## What a session feels like

You arrive, take off your shoes and find a spot. The facilitator usually gives a few short instructions and begins. The volume rises and falls, tones overlap, and the silence between them matters too.

What people report varies a lot:

- A pleasant heaviness, like the moment before sleep.
- Thoughts that settle or go quiet for a while.
- Vibration in the chest or belly from the gong or large bowls.
- Sleepiness. Dozing off is normal.
- Sometimes, emotions that surface without warning.

Cleveland Clinic suggests eating lightly beforehand, drinking water and wearing loose clothes. It also notes that some people leave drowsy and others energized. If you're the drowsy type, give yourself a few minutes before you drive.

## What the research says about sound baths

The honest version: there are few studies, and most are small. These are the ones cited most.

**A 2017 observational study.** Tamara Goldsby and colleagues measured the mood of 62 adults before and after [a Tibetan singing bowl meditation](https://doi.org/10.1177/2156587216668109). Participants reported less tension, anger, fatigue and low mood. First-timers saw a bigger drop in tension. There was no comparison group, so it can't show the bowls caused the change.

**A 2023 randomized trial.** Researchers at Universidad Austral de Chile and the Autonomous University of Barcelona [compared one singing bowl session](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe13020024) with progressive muscle relaxation and a waiting list, in 50 adults with high anxiety. Both methods lowered self-reported anxiety compared with waiting, a little more so with the bowls. The bowl group also showed higher heart rate variability, a measure linked to relaxation.

**A 2020 crossover study.** 48 adults rested for 20 minutes in a hammock hung over a large bowl, once with the bowl struck and once in silence. [With the bowl sounding they rated themselves less sleepy](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0233982), but the objective sleepiness test didn't change.

**The reviews.** A [2020 systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2020.102412) found only four studies and concluded it couldn't yet recommend singing bowl therapy. By 2025, [another review gathered 19 studies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144), nine of them randomized, and spoke of "potential" for anxiety, mood and sleep. A [third 2025 review](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13162002) of 14 studies saw favorable results in most, but the designs were too different to pool. All three call for stronger trials.

Bottom line: there are signs that a sound bath helps you relax in the moment. There's no evidence it cures anything. Be wary of anyone promising to "heal" you with frequencies; that side of sound healing isn't backed by research.

## Who should take care

A sound bath is low risk for most people, but go in with some awareness:

- **Mental health.** Cleveland Clinic suggests checking with your doctor first if you live with a psychiatric condition, since meditation can intensify some symptoms.
- **Other conditions.** If you're pregnant, have epilepsy, tinnitus or strong sensitivity to noise, talk to your doctor and tell the facilitator before you start.
- **Volume.** Sobo notes that playing too loud or with abrupt changes makes it harder to relax. You can ask for a spot farther from the instruments.
- **Your gut.** If something or someone makes you uncomfortable, you can leave. Relaxation doesn't work under pressure.

One more point from Sobo worth keeping: a sound bath won't relieve long-term stress if its causes are still there. It's a pause, not a fix.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need meditation experience for a sound bath?**
No. You just lie down and listen. In the 2017 study, first-timers were the ones whose tension dropped the most.

**What should I bring to a sound bath?**
Comfortable, loose clothes, socks and a layer to stay warm. Eat lightly beforehand and drink water.

**Is sound healing the same as a sound bath?**
People use the terms interchangeably. "Sound healing" suggests a cure, but the evidence only speaks to relaxation and mood in the moment, from small studies.

**Can I fall asleep?**
Yes, and it's common. If it happens, don't worry: that's rest too.

**How long does a session last?**
Most run about an hour, though some are shorter. Ask us how long the sound meditation lasts when you book.
