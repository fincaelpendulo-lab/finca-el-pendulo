If you're wondering **how to start meditating**, start small: sit comfortably, rest your attention on your breath for five minutes, and come back to it each time your mind drifts. You don't need an empty mind or a perfectly still body. Getting distracted isn't failing. Noticing and returning is the practice.

This guide is for anyone who wants to meditate but can't sit still or quiet their thoughts. It covers what meditation is, a first session step by step, a four-week plan, and what the research does and doesn't show.

## What meditation is (and isn't)

The US [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH)](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-what-you-need-to-know) describes meditation as a range of practices that bring mind and body together to calm the mind and support well-being. Some focus attention on one thing, such as the breath, a sound, an image or a mantra. Mindfulness is about noticing the present moment without judging it.

What it isn't: switching off your thoughts. In his [2012 TED talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qzR62JJCMBQ), mindfulness teacher Andy Puddicombe says most people assume meditation is about stopping thoughts, when it's really about stepping back and watching each thought come and go.

A busy mind is the norm, not the exception. In a [2010 Harvard study published in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1192439), Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used a phone app to ask people what they were thinking about during the day. As the [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2010/11/wandering-mind-not-a-happy-mind/) reported, across 2,250 volunteers the mind wandered 46.9 percent of waking hours. Your restless mind isn't a personal flaw. It's a human mind.

## How to start meditating: your first five-minute session

You don't need a special cushion, incense or a lotus pose. The NHS guide [How to meditate for beginners](https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/mental-wellbeing-tips/how-to-meditate-for-beginners/) asks for two things only: that you're comfortable and that you can sit upright. A chair, a mat or the edge of your bed all work.

1. **Pick a regular time.** First thing in the morning or before bed. The same slot each day helps the habit stick.
2. **Set a five-minute timer.** Then you can stop checking the clock.
3. **Sit tall with loose shoulders.** Close your eyes or let your gaze rest on the floor.
4. **Feel your breath.** Don't change it. Notice where you feel it most: nose, chest or belly.
5. **Count from one to ten.** One full breath per number. At ten, start again at one.
6. **When you drift, come back.** If you catch yourself planning dinner, note "thinking" and return to the breath.
7. **Close gently.** Before you open your eyes, listen to the sounds in the room for a few seconds.

Why five minutes and not twenty? In a [New York Times piece republished by The Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/health/your-wellness/2022/09/06/struggling-to-meditate-because-you-cant-sit-still-heres-some-advice-for-the-easily-distracted), Duke University's John Mitchell suggests starting with three to five minutes. The 10 to 30 minute defaults in many apps are probably too long for beginners, especially anyone who struggles to focus.

## A four-week plan to build the habit

This plan is our suggestion at the Finca for growing the habit slowly. Adjust it to your life: consistency matters more than minutes.

| Week | Time per day | What to practise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5 minutes | Count breaths from one to ten, at the same time each day |
| 2 | 5 to 7 minutes | Silently name what pulls you away ("thinking", "planning") and return |
| 3 | 8 to 10 minutes | Alternate: one day seated, the next walking slowly and counting steps |
| 4 | 10 to 13 minutes | Try a guided meditation and one session outdoors |

How long should you meditate each day? A [2019 New York University trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbr.2018.08.023) gave adults with no meditation experience either 13 minutes a day of guided meditation or a podcast. After eight weeks, though not after four, the meditation group had better mood, attention and memory, and less anxiety during a stress test. It's one study, but it suggests the first month is a beginning, not the finish line.

Guided meditation is good company early on: a voice reminds you to come back to the breath. Later, the NHS suggests 20 minutes as a useful guide, though there's no fixed rule.

## A restless mind is normal: common beginner mistakes

Most early stumbles come from expecting something meditation doesn't promise. These are the usual ones:

- **Thinking you're doing it wrong because you're thinking.** The NHS is clear that a wandering mind is a natural part of practice, not a failure. If you wander off ten times, you come back ten times.
- **Fighting your thoughts.** The [NHS mindfulness page](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/tips-and-support/mindfulness/) suggests treating them as passing mental events and naming them silently, for example: "this is anxiety".
- **Starting with long sessions.** Five minutes every day carries you further than an hour on Sunday and nothing else all week.
- **Being hard on yourself.** As Dan Harris puts it in the same Irish Times piece, distraction in meditation is not proof of failure.
- **Forcing stillness.** If you really can't stay seated, move. The NHS mentions gentle yoga and walking as good options when your mind feels busy. Walking while counting your steps is meditation too.

## What research says about meditation

The most cited review is a [2014 Johns Hopkins meta-analysis in *JAMA Internal Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13018). It pooled 47 trials with 3,515 participants. Mindfulness programmes showed moderate evidence of improved anxiety, depression and pain, and low evidence for stress. The authors describe small to moderate reductions, and they found no evidence that meditation beat other active treatments such as exercise or therapy.

The NCCIH adds an honest caveat: much of the research is preliminary and may have been read too optimistically. Meditation is a wellbeing practice. It doesn't replace medical or psychological care.

It also doesn't suit everyone. A [2020 systematic review in *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*](https://doi.org/10.1111/acps.13225) looked at 83 studies and found that about 8 percent of people who meditated reported a negative experience, most often anxiety or low mood. If you live with anxiety, depression or trauma, or you're in treatment, talk to your doctor or therapist before you start and look for a trained teacher.

One of the best-known programmes is mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), which Jon Kabat-Zinn developed at the clinic he founded at the University of Massachusetts in 1979. His book [*Full Catastrophe Living*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/89149/full-catastrophe-living-revised-edition-by-jon-kabat-zinn/) (Bantam, revised 2013) lays out the programme and is a good next read.

## Meditating in a group and in nature

If you find it hard to keep going alone, the Irish Times piece suggests getting support: a group or someone with training. Place matters too. In a [2015 Stanford study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1510459112), a 90-minute walk in a natural setting reduced rumination, that loop of the same thoughts over and over, while a walk through the city did not. ## Frequently asked questions

**How many minutes a day should a beginner meditate?**
Start with three to five minutes and build up slowly. A 2019 study of adults new to meditation used 13 minutes a day and saw changes after eight weeks. A little every day beats a lot once in a while.

**Is it normal not to be able to stop thinking?**
Yes. The mind wanders almost half the time, in meditation and out of it. The exercise isn't to silence it but to notice you've drifted and return to the breath.

**Should I start with guided meditation or silence?**
Both work. Guided sessions help at first because a voice reminds you to come back. Once the habit is in place, try alternating with silent sessions.

**Can I meditate lying down?**
You can, though it's easier to fall asleep. The NHS recommends a comfortable, upright posture; if your back hurts, a chair with a backrest works well.
